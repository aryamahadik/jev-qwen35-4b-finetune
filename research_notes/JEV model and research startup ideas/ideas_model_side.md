# Model, Architecture and Inference-Algorithm Startup Ideas Within a ~$30 GPU Budget (vetted as of 2026-09-25)

Scope: new model, architecture and inference-algorithm ideas that a student team could run on about $30 of Modal GPU, that would give publishable benchmark numbers in an under-explored space, and that could lead to a startup. I evaluated 17 candidates. Three survive: two main ideas and one idea adjacent to the team's decision/probability model. The other 14 are logged with the prior work that rules them out.

## Q1. Overall verdict: which candidates survive, and what does $30 actually buy?

### Takeaway
Most agent-inference ideas from the brief were already done in 2026: KV-cache reuse for tool schemas, speculative or parallel tool-call decoding, diffusion LMs for JSON, tool reward models, calibration, looped LMs for tool calling, and tool-doc-to-LoRA hypernetworks. The clearest open space is **applying tiny recursive models (TRM/PTRM) to real grid-shaped business data that isn't a puzzle**. Three ideas survive: **(1) SheetTRM**, a recursive grid model that finds tables and cell roles in spreadsheets; **(2) FlashTRM**, a recursive model that learns spreadsheet string transforms from examples; and **(3) SpanCall**, an encoder-only, single-pass function-calling model (the adjacent idea). By startup potential the ranking is SheetTRM, then SpanCall, then FlashTRM.

### Cited Findings
- Modal list prices (Sept 2026): H100 about $3.95/h, A100-80GB about $2.50/h, A100-40GB about $2.10/h, L40S about $1.95/h. So $30 buys about 7.6 H100-hours, 12 A100-80GB-hours or 15.4 L40S-hours. — [Modal pricing](https://modal.com/pricing) (as compiled in the team's `compute_budget_and_funding_playbook.md`)
- TRM reference training costs from the official repo: Sudoku-Extreme (1,000 examples, 50,000 epochs, 7M params) takes "< 20 hours" on 1 L40S. Maze-Hard takes "< 24 hours" on 4 L40S. ARC-AGI-1/2 take "~3 days" on 4 H100s. The repo was archived read-only on Apr 1, 2026. — [SamsungSAILMontreal/TinyRecursiveModels](https://github.com/SamsungSAILMontreal/TinyRecursiveModels)
- TRM (7M params) reaches 45% on ARC-AGI-1 and 8% on ARC-AGI-2 with a single 2-layer network that repeatedly refines a latent z and an answer y. — [Jolicoeur-Martineau, arXiv 2510.04871](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04871)
- PTRM adds Gaussian noise at each deep-recursion step, runs parallel trajectories, and picks one with the existing Q (halting) head. With no retraining, Sudoku-Extreme rises from 87.4% to 98.75% and Pencil Puzzle Bench from 62.6% to 91.2%, against 55.1% for frontier LLMs. — [PTRM, arXiv 2605.19943](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19943)
- Commentators note that TRM "is mainly tested on structured reasoning (Sudoku, Maze, ARC)" and has not been shown on open-ended language. — [TRM explainer (intoai.pub)](https://www.intoai.pub/p/tiny-recursive-model); [TRM paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.04871)

### Inferences
- **Summary scorecard of survivors** (novelty / feasibility / measurability / market / crowding):
  - SheetTRM: novel method (no TRM on native spreadsheets; closest is FastTab, which works on table images) / fits $30 only with a cut-down TRM recipe / public benchmarks: TableSense annotations and StatSheets / clear buyers in finance and insurance data ops / crowding at the method level is low, at the product level moderate.
  - FlashTRM: novel (no TRM for programming-by-example found) / fits easily because data is synthetic / PROSE and SyGuS benchmarks with published LLM numbers / weakest market pull / low research crowding.
  - SpanCall (adjacent): moderately novel (encoder-only function calling on BFCL not found) / cheap (~$10-20) / BFCL with published small-model numbers / strong fit with the team's decision model / "fast function calling" is crowded, but encoder-only is not.
- The user's "20-30 A100-hours" is more than Modal list prices allow. At list price $30 is about 12-14 A100-hours, so plan on about 7.5 H100-hours or 15 L40S-hours.
- A full TRM training recipe (50k epochs) is **over $30** for grids bigger than Sudoku. Sudoku costs about 20 L40S-hours (~$39). Maze-Hard, a 30x30 grid, costs about 96 L40S-hours (~$187). Both TRM-based survivors therefore need a cut-down recipe: fewer epochs, fewer recursions, axial attention, or large synthetic datasets instead of heavy augmentation over 1,000 examples.

### Gaps
- I did not check stealth startups on Crunchbase or X in depth. Competitor lists come from arXiv, GitHub, Hugging Face and web search.
- Modal prices are from the team's sibling notes, not re-fetched here.

## Q2. Surviving idea 1: SheetTRM, a tiny recursive grid model for spreadsheet table detection and cell-type classification

### Takeaway
No one has applied TRM/PTRM-style recursion to native spreadsheet grids (.xlsx cell layouts). The closest work, FastTab, uses a TRM module for table recognition on *images*. Public benchmarks exist with clear numbers to beat: **SpreadsheetLLM reports 78.9% F1 on table detection**, and a CRF-LightGBM system scores **0.937 file-macro F1 on cell-type classification on StatSheets**. A 5-10M-parameter model could plausibly beat an LLM-based system on CPU, and there is real buyer pain around messy spreadsheets.

### Cited Findings
- **SOTA to beat (table detection):** SpreadsheetLLM's "fine-tuned LLM with SheetCompressor" reaches "a state-of-the-art 78.9% F1 score, surpassing the best existing models by 12.3%". The abstract does not name the base model. — [SpreadsheetLLM, arXiv 2407.09025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.09025)
- A secondary summary says GPT-4 with SheetCompressor (in-context) reached F1 0.759, and 0.718 on large sheets versus 0.288 without compression. — [SpreadsheetLLM review (themoonlight.io)](https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/spreadsheetllm-encoding-spreadsheets-for-large-language-models) (secondary; confirm against the paper's tables)
- **Public data:** TableSense publishes table-range annotations for VEnron2 (12,254 sheets), VEUSES (363) and VFUSE (1,143). Each sheet was labeled by at least two people, leaving 2,615 tables. — [microsoft/TableSense](https://github.com/microsoft/TableSense); [HF mirror kl3269/tablesense](https://huggingface.co/datasets/kl3269/tablesense); [TableSense paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13500)
- **SOTA to beat (cell-type classification):** a CRF-LightGBM system (65 features plus a pairwise CRF over the grid) gets 0.937 mean file-macro F1 on StatSheets, "within 0.6 percentage points of the GPU-based TUTA Transformer". It uses a deterministic table-detection stage that is "competitive with ... SpreadsheetLLM". StatSheets has 737 manually annotated multilingual sheets from 14 providers and the authors say they "share" it. — [arXiv 2608.16050 (Aug 2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16050)
- TUTA reports 88.1% average macro-F1 for cell-type classification across WebSheet, SAUS, DeEx and CIUS. — [TUTA, arXiv 2010.12537](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.12537). A GitHub issue shows users asking for the WebSheet, SAUS and CIUS data, so availability is uncertain. — [TUTA issue #17](https://github.com/microsoft/TUTA_table_understanding/issues/17)
- **Closest architectural prior:** FastTab combines "a lightweight Tiny Recursive Module (TRM) for global reasoning" with axial 1D transformers for table structure recognition on *document images* (PubTabNet, FinTabNet, PubTables-1M, SciTSR). It does not handle native spreadsheets; code is "will be made publicly available". — [FastTab, arXiv 2605.22422](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.22422)
- Other active spreadsheet work: SheetCompass (hierarchical relation graphs for agentic spreadsheet reasoning). — [arXiv 2608.14452](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.14452)
- **Market pull:** LlamaIndex has a Spreadsheet Agent in private preview that turns messy spreadsheets into normalized 2D tables and handles merged cells. — [LlamaIndex blog](https://www.llamaindex.ai/blog/introducing-the-spreadsheet-agent-in-private-preview); [LlamaIndex "Best AI for messy spreadsheets"](https://www.llamaindex.ai/insights/best-ai-for-messy-spreadsheets). Querri detects headers, footer totals, merged cells and multiple tables per sheet. — [Querri](https://querri.com/blog/5-ai-tools-for-excel-that-will-save-you-hours-every-week/). Unstract offers AI extraction from Excel. — [Unstract](https://unstract.com/blog/extract-data-from-excel-documents-with-ai-unstract-excel-document-processing/)
- PTRM-style "verifier-augmented" inference for "document validation, forms checking, and structured extraction with partial verifiers" appears as future work on Emergent Mind's page for PTRM. This may be Emergent Mind's own summary rather than the paper's text. — [Emergent Mind PTRM page](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2605.19943)

### Inferences
- **One-line pitch:** "A ~7M-parameter recursive grid model that reads raw .xlsx layout and finds tables and header/data/total cells more accurately than a fine-tuned-LLM system, on a CPU, for a tiny fraction of the cost per workbook."
- **Core technical insight:** Finding tables and cell roles is grid labeling with global constraints. Headers sit above data blocks, tables are rectangles, totals rows close a block, and blank bands separate tables. That is the regime where TRM wins (Sudoku/Maze: local evidence plus global consistency, little data). Current systems either classify cells one-shot (LightGBM+CRF, the CNN in TableSense) or serialize and compress the sheet for an LLM (SpreadsheetLLM). SheetTRM instead refines a per-cell label map y and a latent z over several steps, with deep supervision and a Q-head that decides when to stop. At test time, PTRM-style noisy trajectories are scored by the Q-head plus a cheap structural checker (rectangular tables, header above data, consistent types down a column). Combining a hand-written checker with the Q-head is itself a small new contribution.
- **Exact experiment:**
  1. Featurize each cell with openpyxl: type (empty/str/num/date/formula/error), number format, bold/italic/size, fill, 4 borders, merge flags, alignment and indent. Add a 32-64-dim projection of a frozen small text-encoder embedding of the cell string.
  2. Use 64x32 windows with axial (row/column) attention, and overlapping sliding windows plus stitching for big sheets.
  3. Heads: a per-cell cell-type label and a table-membership/boundary mask. Get table boxes from connected components.
  4. Train on the TableSense annotations and StatSheets (5-fold CV to match 2608.16050). Augment with blank row/column insertion, style jitter, value shuffling within columns, and synthetic multi-table sheets built by tiling real tables.
  5. Report: (a) table-detection F1 using SpreadsheetLLM's exact protocol and split, against 78.9%; (b) cell-type file-macro F1 on StatSheets, against 0.937 (CRF-LightGBM) and about 0.943 (TUTA); (c) ablations: 1 recursion step vs N, with and without PTRM and the checker, and a same-features LightGBM, to show the gain comes from recursion and not the features; (d) CPU latency and $/1k sheets against LLM token cost.
- **Baseline to beat:** 78.9% table-detection F1 (SpreadsheetLLM, fine-tuned) and 0.937 cell-type F1 (CRF-LightGBM on StatSheets).
- **Compute estimate:** The model is tiny, but TRM's usual 50k-epoch recipe on ~2k-token grids would cost roughly $150-200 (compare Maze-Hard at about 96 L40S-hours). Cut-down plan: T=2/n=4 recursions, bf16, axial attention, about 2-5k epochs-equivalent with early stopping. Main run about 4 H100-hours (~$16), ablations about 2-3 H100-hours (~$8-12), embedding precompute and evaluation under 0.5 H100-hours. **Total about $25-30 (tight).** Featurization runs free on a laptop CPU.
- **Novelty and crowding verdict:** SURVIVES. I found no TRM or recursive-latent model for native spreadsheet table detection or cell-type classification. Method-level crowding is low: Microsoft (TableSense, TUTA, SpreadsheetLLM), the 2608.16050 group and FastTab (image modality). Product-level crowding is moderate: LlamaIndex, Unstract, Querri, and presumably Copilot in Excel.
- **Main risks:**
  - The cell-type task is near its ceiling (0.937), so the headline has to be table detection or cost.
  - Comparisons are only valid if the split and metric match SpreadsheetLLM's exactly.
  - The data is small and varied, so overfitting is likely.
  - The budget is tight for TRM-style training.
  - Reviewers will ask whether recursion or the features did the work, so the ablation is required.
  - Microsoft could release a better spreadsheet model.
- **Startup angle:** A "spreadsheet ingestion micro-model", sold as an API and an on-prem CPU binary that sits in front of LLM agents and ETL. It turns messy workbooks into clean tables plus header metadata, and cuts LLM token spend by sending only the detected tables. Likely first customers: data-ops teams at insurers and reinsurers ingesting bordereaux, FP&A consolidation teams, statistics aggregators and data vendors. It can also be sold as a component to spreadsheet-agent builders. The same grid backbone could later add FlashTRM (Q3) and formula prediction.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm SpreadsheetLLM's exact table-detection metric (e.g., EoB-0 vs EoB-2), its test split, or its base model from the abstract. Read Section 4 and the appendix before claiming a win.
- StatSheets' download URL and license were not verified. The abstract only says the authors "share" it.
- Whether the DeEx, SAUS, CIUS and WebSheet data can be obtained is uncertain (see the TUTA issue).
- Whether FastTab's code has actually been released was not verified.

## Q3. Surviving idea 2: FlashTRM, a tiny recursive model for spreadsheet string transforms by example (text-editing PBE)

### Takeaway
Text-editing programming-by-example (PBE) is "ARC for strings". The best published LLM result, a fine-tuned 33B model, reaches **82% generalization accuracy on 100 PROSE problems**, but only after tens to hundreds of samples, which means minutes per problem on an A6000. Excel's FlashFill scores **33%**. I found no TRM or recursive-latent model applied to PBE. A 7M-parameter recursive model trained on unlimited synthetic data could make a clean "7M vs 33B, milliseconds vs minutes" paper. Its standalone market pull is the weakest of the three survivors.

### Cited Findings
- On 100 string-to-string problems from the Microsoft PROSE benchmark (up to 10 I/O examples, at least one held out): FlashFill (Excel for Mac) gets **33%** generalization accuracy, FlashFill++ reports about 100% *oracle* accuracy only, and the fine-tuned DeepSeekCoder-33B gets **82% generalization / 88% oracle**. — [Li & Ellis, "Is Programming by Example solved by LLMs?", NeurIPS 2024, arXiv 2406.08316](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.08316) (Table 1 and Appendix A.1.2)
- The same paper says the fine-tuned model "essentially never produces a correct program on the first try. It requires tens or hundreds of samples", and rejection sampling takes "on the order of a few minutes to solve a given problem" on an A6000. — [arXiv 2406.08316](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.08316)
- Out-of-distribution: a model seeded with PROSE problems scores 76% on other PROSE problems, but only 59% when seeded with the easier SyGuS problems. — [arXiv 2406.08316](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.08316)
- The benchmark suite is public at github.com/microsoft/prose-benchmarks. The paper notes that FlashFill "ships in Microsoft Excel and is used by many millions of people". — [arXiv 2406.08316](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.08316)
- Recent LLM-agent work on PBE continues. — ["From I/O to Code with Discovery Agent", arXiv 2605.15334](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.15334)
- A search for tiny recursive models applied to programming-by-example or string transformation found only general TRM explainers and classic PBE/ILP papers, no TRM-for-PBE work. — [search results incl. TRM repo](https://github.com/SamsungSAILMontreal/TinyRecursiveModels); [SyGuS-Comp 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.07627)
- TRM's ARC recipe (transduction, recursion, deep supervision, heavy augmentation) and PTRM's multi-trajectory selection are the architectural templates. — [TRM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04871); [PTRM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19943)

### Inferences
- **One-line pitch:** "A 7M-parameter recursive model that completes a spreadsheet column from 2-3 examples in milliseconds, aiming to match the 82% a fine-tuned 33B LLM gets on PROSE after minutes of sampling."
- **Core technical insight:**
  - Treat PBE as ARC-style *transduction*: lay out the I/O demos as a character grid (rows are examples, columns are character positions) and predict the query's output directly with recursive refinement.
  - PBE comes with a **free, label-free verifier**: leave-one-out on the demos (hide one demo and predict it). That lets PTRM's noisy trajectories be ranked by something better than the Q-head alone, which fills the gap the PTRM authors point to.
  - A FlashFill-style DSL generator gives unlimited synthetic training tasks. That avoids TRM's 1,000-example, 50k-epoch overfitting regime, which is also why it fits the budget.
- **Exact experiment:**
  1. Generate 5-20M synthetic tasks on CPU from a DSL: substrings, regex-token positions, case, concatenation, constants, date and number formats.
  2. Train TRM-Att (~7M params) with up to 4 demos, strings of 48 characters or fewer, 16 deep-supervision steps.
  3. Evaluate on the 100 PROSE problems using Li & Ellis's protocol, plus SyGuS PBE-strings. Metric: generalization accuracy (all held-out outputs correct).
  4. Also run Qwen3-1.7B/4B few-shot and report latency for everything.
  5. Ablate recursion depth, test-time augmentation (permuting demo order), and PTRM with vs without the leave-one-out verifier.
- **Baseline to beat:** 82% generalization accuracy on PROSE (fine-tuned DeepSeekCoder-33B). Beating Excel FlashFill's 33% is the floor. The 59% out-of-distribution figure is a fair comparison point for a model trained only on synthetic data.
- **Compute estimate:** data generation is free on CPU. Training about 4-6 H100-hours (~$16-24). Evaluation and LLM baselines about 1 H100-hour (~$4). **Total about $20-28.**
- **Novelty and crowding verdict:** SURVIVES. No TRM or recursive-latent PBE model found. Research crowding is low to moderate (Microsoft PROSE, Li & Ellis at Cornell, LLM-agent PBE work). The product space is dominated by Excel Flash Fill (free), Google Sheets and LLM copilots.
- **Main risks:**
  - PROSE includes noisy and world-knowledge tasks (e.g., month names, lookups) that a 7M model trained from scratch cannot know, so the realistic ceiling may be below 82%.
  - Transduction produces no program, so there is no audit trail and the model must run per row, though at 7M params that is cheap.
  - Reviewers may treat FlashFill++'s ~100% oracle accuracy as the true SOTA, so the claim has to be precisely "generalization accuracy per millisecond".
  - The synthetic-to-real gap.
- **Startup angle:** Weak on its own because Excel already has Flash Fill. It is strongest as a feature inside SheetTRM's ingestion product: auto-normalizing columns (dates, IDs, names, codes) during ingestion, running offline or on-prem for regulated industries where LLM calls are not allowed. It is publishable quickly and adds credibility to a "tiny recursive models for spreadsheets" thesis.

### Gaps
- I did not extract exact SyGuS numbers or the per-size results (1.3B/7B/33B) from Li & Ellis's figures. Only Table 1 (33B on PROSE) was confirmed.
- I did not verify RobustFill's numbers on this 100-problem PROSE subset. The paper plots RobustFill for the list domain via [17].

## Q4. Surviving idea 3 (adjacent to the decision model): SpanCall, encoder-only single-pass function calling

### Takeaway
Every "fast function calling" system I found still decodes with a decoder LLM: ToolSpec (4x), SimpleTool/RealtimeTool (3-6x, 61.2 ms P50 at 4B), HyFunc (0.828 s, 80.1% BFCL), OoO-Spec and FunctionGemma (270M). I found **no public encoder-only, GLiNER2-style model that picks the tool and extracts arguments in one pass and reports BFCL numbers**. The numbers to beat for small models on BFCL non-live *simple* AST are **Qwen3-0.6B 60.47%, Qwen3-1.7B 75.19% and Qwen3-4B 84.50%**. Novelty is moderate (it is classic intent+slot filling applied to JSON-schema tools), but it fits the team's probability-output model well.

### Cited Findings
- BFCL non-live Simple AST accuracy: Qwen3-4B (prompt) 84.50%, Qwen3-1.7B (prompt) 75.19%, xLAM-2-3b-fc-r 74.03%, a 1B xLAM model 67.83% (named "xLAM-3-1b-fc-r" in the snippet, possibly a typo for xLAM-2-1b-fc-r), Qwen3-0.6B (prompt) 60.47%. — [TinyLLM, arXiv 2511.22138, Table II](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.22138)
- SimpleTool/RealtimeTool (ICML 2026) uses special tokens to compress low-entropy tokens 4-6x and generate the function name and arguments in parallel. It reports 3-6x end-to-end speedup (up to 9.6x) and 61.2 ms P50 at 4B, and ST-Qwen-0.5B beats FunctionGemma on Mobile Actions. — [arXiv 2603.00030](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00030); [ICML poster](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/poster/62617)
- HyFunc cascades a large model (intent to soft token) into a retriever and a small prefix-tuned model, with "dynamic templating" in vLLM: 0.828 s latency and 80.1% on BFCL. Code is public. — [arXiv 2602.13665](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.13665)
- ToolSpec fills deterministic schema tokens with a finite-state machine and speculates only on variable fields, for about a 4x speedup. — [arXiv 2604.13519](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13519v1). OoO-Spec speculates the function and arguments out of order. — [arXiv 2608.00814](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.00814)
- FunctionGemma (270M, Gemma 3 based): 58% out of the box and 85% after fine-tuning on the Mobile Actions task. — [FunctionGemma model card](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/docs/functiongemma/model_card); [Ertas AI blog](https://www.ertas.ai/blog/functiongemma-dedicated-tool-calling-models)
- Encoder precedents: GLiNER2 does schema-conditioned extraction and classification of all labels "in a single forward pass". — [GLiNER2, arXiv 2507.18546](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.18546); [fastino-ai/GLiNER2](https://github.com/fastino-ai/GLiNER2). GLiNER-Relex reports 0.9 s per document against 64 s for GPT-5-mini, about 70x throughput. — [arXiv 2605.10108](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.10108)
- TinyAgent uses a DeBERTa-v3-small multi-label classifier for tool retrieval, then a 1.1B/7B LLM generates the call. — [TinyAgent, arXiv 2409.00608](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.00608v3). Octopus v2 uses "functional tokens" in a decoder. — [arXiv 2404.01744](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.01744v1). Span-based slot filling for dialog is well established. — [Span-ConveRT, ACL 2020](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.11/)
- Public training data: the APIGen pipeline and its verifiable function-calling dataset (xLAM-60k). — [APIGen, arXiv 2406.18518](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.18518)

### Inferences
- **One-line pitch:** "Function calls in one encoder forward pass: a schema-conditioned ModernBERT picks the tool and fills arguments with calibrated per-field probabilities, and calls a small LLM only when an argument has to be generated."
- **Core technical insight:** In BFCL's AST categories, tool choice is classification. Most argument values are spans copied from the query, enum members, booleans or numbers. Function calling is therefore zero-shot, schema-conditioned joint intent detection and slot filling, which GLiNER2-style encoders do orders of magnitude faster than autoregressive decoding. Unlike decoder-based speedups, the encoder outputs a calibrated probability for every field. That directly feeds the team's decision/probability model and gives per-argument abstention.
- **Exact experiment:**
  1. Encoder: ModernBERT-base, or start from a GLiNER2 checkpoint. Input: [query] [SEP] [tool name + description] [param_i name/type/description]...
  2. Heads:
     - per-tool relevance (sigmoid, which also covers "irrelevance");
     - per-parameter type switch: span, enum, bool, number, null, or needs-generation;
     - span start/end pointer;
     - enum classifier over value embeddings;
     - number/date normalizer.
  3. Parallel calls use per-tool instance slots. The fallback is Qwen3-0.6B/1.7B, constrained to fill only the flagged arguments.
  4. Build training labels by aligning xLAM-60k argument values to query spans. The alignment rate is a first finding in itself, and a cheap CPU-only one. Add synthetic irrelevant-tool negatives.
  5. Evaluate on BFCL v3/v4 non-live AST (simple, multiple, parallel, parallel-multiple), irrelevance, and live simple/multiple. Report accuracy, fallback rate, p50/p95 latency on CPU and GPU, and ECE of the field probabilities.
- **Baseline to beat:** simple AST 75.19% (Qwen3-1.7B) at 10x or more lower latency, with 84.50% (Qwen3-4B) as the stretch goal once fallback is on. Latency references: SimpleTool 61.2 ms P50 (4B) and HyFunc 0.828 s.
- **Compute estimate:** encoder fine-tuning about 1-2 H100-hours ($4-8). Optional LoRA for the fallback about 1-2 H100-hours. Running baseline LLMs on BFCL about 1 H100-hour. **Total about $10-20.**
- **Novelty and crowding verdict:** SURVIVES as the one adjacent idea, with moderate novelty. The encoder-only approach on BFCL appears unclaimed. The wider "fast or small function calling" space is crowded: Google (FunctionGemma), Salesforce (xLAM), the SimpleTool, HyFunc, ToolSpec and OoO-Spec authors, Nexa AI (Octopus), Berkeley (TinyAgent). The most likely fast follower is Fastino (GLiNER2's maintainer), which could ship this quickly.
- **Main risks:**
  - Many BFCL arguments need computing or normalizing (units, lists, nested dicts), which could push the fallback rate high and erase the latency gain.
  - Span-aligned labels from xLAM will be noisy.
  - BFCL v4's overall score weights agentic and multi-turn categories where this approach does not apply, so claims must be limited to the AST categories.
  - It overlaps the other researcher's decision model and should be framed as that model's argument-filling stage.
- **Startup angle:** A "tool-call co-processor" that handles routine calls in about 10 ms and escalates the rest. Buyers are latency-critical: voice agents, real-time or robotic control (SimpleTool's own motivation is 16 Hz control), and on-device assistants. It pairs naturally with the team's decision model.

### Gaps
- I have not measured what fraction of BFCL AST argument values appear verbatim in the query. This decides whether SpanCall works and should be checked first (it is free).
- There is a small chance an encoder-only BFCL result sits in a workshop paper or on the Hugging Face hub under another name. My searches for GLiNER-style, encoder-only and span-extraction function calling found none.

## Q5. Dropped-idea log: candidates rejected and who already did them

### Takeaway
14 candidates were dropped. Most failed on novelty: KV-cache reuse for tool schemas, speculative or parallel tool-call decoding, diffusion LMs for structured output, tool reward and verifier models, calibration, looped LMs, hypernetwork LoRA for tool docs, and activation steering all have 2025-2026 papers, often with code and benchmarks. A few failed on feasibility or crowding: TRM for TSP, test-time-scaling ideas, schema matching and tabular error detection.

### Cited Findings
1. **Tool-schema KV-cache compression or reuse for agents (DROPPED: done).** ReCache (Aug 2026) caches tool and skill schemas independently with "resource-wise attention" because schemas recur "in different combinations and orders", which breaks prefix caching. — [ReCache, arXiv 2608.19662](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19662). Also covered: learned KV management for agentic serving. — [arXiv 2608.14624](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14624); stateful inference for multi-agent tool calling. — [arXiv 2605.26289](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.26289)

### Inferences
- The pattern: anything that makes *LLM agents* faster or more reliable got many papers between late 2025 and Q3 2026, often from big labs (Google, Salesforce, IBM, Sakana, ByteDance) with code. A $30 team cannot win those races.
- The openings are where a **new architecture (TRM/PTRM) meets an old, business-relevant structured-data task** that has public benchmarks and weak or expensive LLM baselines. Spreadsheet structure and string transforms by example fit, which is why both main survivors are there.
- Item 13 (schema matching) is the best candidate to revisit if the team wants a third TRM paper. Its market is strong (data onboarding and mapping), but it has to beat CGM's constraint-gated small model.
- For the other researcher's decision model: the dropped calibration and verifier work (items 5 and 6) is directly relevant prior art to cite. SpanCall (Q4) is designed to be that model's argument-filling stage.
