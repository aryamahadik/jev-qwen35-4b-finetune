Technical report · 26 September 2026

One open 4B model beats Jev on its own benchmark

OpenDecide v0.10 is a single Qwen3.5-4B with a LoRA adapter that answers by reading option logits in one forward pass. On all 231 public JevBench items it scores higher than TypeSafe's Jev on every official axis: intelligence, calibration, speed and cost. On a 30-metric decision suite it wins most metrics with confidence intervals that exclude zero. End to end it is 1.7 times faster.

Selection used only dev splits and JevBench half A. Half B was run once, at the end. Jev was used for evaluation only and never as a training source.

Speed

A 4B decision on an H100 costs about 10 ms of arithmetic, but the eager PyTorch path spent roughly 55 ms per call on kernel launches. We capture the forward pass once per 64-token length bucket as a CUDA graph and replay it. Inputs are right-padded without an attention mask, which is exact because attention, the Gated DeltaNet recurrence and the short convolution are all causal. A start-up self-check measured a maximum probability difference of 0.0 against the eager path.

Leverage-bounded decoding

The decoder rereads the input with each short segment removed. It drops a segment only if removing it flips the answer and the same model judges that segment to be a claim that dictates the answer. On v0.7 it raised attacked accuracy on the audit from 39.1 to 64.4 and cut attack success from 79% to 34%. On the untrained model it hurt (94 → 80 on half A), because the claim check had not been trained. After debate training, the model resists the attack by itself, and at the dev-chosen threshold the decoder changes almost nothing (90.7 plain vs 90.1). The fast configuration is therefore the headline.

Method

  • One model. Qwen3.5-4B (Gated DeltaNet plus attention hybrid), LoRA rank 16 on every projection, merged for serving. No cascade, no fallback, no generation: the answer distribution is a softmax over the option-letter logits after one forward pass.
  • Loss. Cross-entropy plus consistency terms (a question and its negation must sum to one; paraphrases and attacked copies must agree with their clean twins), a KL anchor to the frozen base on classification rows, and soft targets where exact probabilities are known.
  • Data. Public datasets, exact procedurally generated decisions, and items written by an open teacher (Qwen3-235B) kept only when two independent verifiers agreed. No Jev output was ever used for training.
  • Hygiene. Rows sharing any 10-word run with JevBench public text were removed (31 procedural, 2 LLM-authored). Every eval and dev state is asserted absent from training. Debate data excludes all audit titles and any 10-word overlap with audit text.
  • Selection. Stages, temperatures and decoding thresholds were chosen on dev splits and JevBench half A only, using rules written down before looking at the eval sets.

Limits

  • The JevBench accuracy lead (+4 items of 231) is within noise; the axis wins in calibration, speed and cost are larger and structural.
  • Five hard JevBench items share concepts with examples in my procedural-generator spec. Without them: 199 vs 197 and 196.
  • The debate training data comes from the same kind of source as the audit (Wikipedia deletion discussions), with the audit's own articles removed. It is in-domain data for that test.
  • JevBench has no private tier here; the judge and sealed tiers could not be run.
  • Jev is ahead on paraphrase drift, the echo-control yes/no subset, one unseen negation wording in zero-shot, and one easy JevBench item.

Cost of the whole study

About $22–23 of Modal GPU credit (billing lags, so the final figure may move slightly) and $1.50 of OpenRouter API spend for data generation and verification. Training all four stages took about 2.8 H100-hours.
